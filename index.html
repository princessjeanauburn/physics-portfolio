chaos Theory

Chaos theory studies deterministic systems whose long-term behaviour appears unpredictable. Although the governing equations are completely known, tiny differences in the initial conditions grow rapidly over time, producing dramatically different trajectories. This phenomenon is known as sensitivity to initial conditions, or the Butterfly Effect.

The Lorenz and Rössler attractors are classic examples of chaotic systems and demonstrate how simple nonlinear equations can generate complex, seemingly random behaviour.

🌪️ Why is the Lorenz System Complex?

The Lorenz system is considered a complex system because it consists of three coupled nonlinear differential equations whose interactions produce emergent behaviour that cannot be understood from any single variable alone.

  • Nonlinearity: Variables continuously influence one another.
  • Sensitive dependence: Tiny changes in the initial state produce completely different trajectories.
  • Deterministic chaos: The equations are deterministic, yet long-term prediction becomes practically impossible.

Rather than repeating periodically, the trajectory evolves within the butterfly-shaped strange attractor, illustrating ordered chaos.

📈 Lorenz Return Map

A return map is a discrete representation of a continuous chaotic system. Here, each local maximum of the Lorenz z-variable is plotted against the following maximum (Zn versus Zn+1).

Scatter plot of successive maxima with the diagonal reference line y = x.

Instead of collapsing onto a single point, the maxima form a nonlinear curve, demonstrating deterministic chaos and the absence of simple periodic behavior.

🕸️ Logistic Map & Cobweb Diagram

The logistic map is one of the simplest mathematical models capable of producing chaotic behaviour. The cobweb diagram visualizes repeated iterations of the nonlinear equation xₙ₊₁ = rxₙ(1 − xₙ), allowing the evolution of the system to be followed step by step.

Animated cobweb diagram showing successive iterations between the logistic curve and the diagonal line y = x.

For r = 3.8, the iterations do not converge to a fixed point, revealing deterministic chaos and strong sensitivity to initial conditions.
